How Pick'Em Picker Works

You're in Straight-up mode — the table ranks each game by the favorite's chance of winning outright. Winning by any number of points counts the same.

You're in ATS (against the spread) mode — the table ranks each game by which side is most likely to beat its posted point spread.

You're in Survivor mode — pick one team to win outright each week, and you cannot use that team again. A loss or tie eliminates the entry.

🏈 How Survivor picks work

Each entry picks one team to win outright each week and can never use that team again. Once a week's games have results, the week locks and any entry whose pick lost or tied is eliminated; the two cards below then digest the remaining schedule into advice for your entry.

Best survival path chooses a legal sequence of weekly picks that maximizes your chance of lasting through the remaining schedule. It may pass on this week's largest favorite when saving that team for a much better later matchup improves the full path.

Best win-the-pool play also considers active opponents' real used teams. It estimates how likely each available team is to be selected under the Chalk, Balanced, or Contrarian setting, then simulates 10,000 seasons. The leverage choice can differ from the safest choice when a less popular winner gives you a better chance to outlast the pool.

🧭 The weekly routine

  1. Set up once, at the start of the season. Name your entry and each opponent, then record what every entry has picked in any weeks already played. The pool needs each entry's history — not just yours — to know who is still alive and to plan sensibly.
  2. Each week, make every active entry's pick. One team to win outright per entry, per week. A team can only be used once all season, and a team on a bye that week is not an option, so each pick comes from the teams that are both still fresh and playing. Update opponents' picks the same way.
  3. Let the results settle the pool. When a week's games have outcomes, that week locks: entries whose team lost or tied are eliminated and everyone else continues. The pool keeps showing each entry as they were at every earlier week, so you always know who was still alive when.
  4. Let the app plan ahead. Choose the public-behavior preset that best matches how your opponents tend to pick, calculate strategy, and the two cards tell you which team maximizes your chance of surviving the rest of the season and which maximizes your chance of winning the pool. Recalculate whenever the roster, any pick, or the preset changes.
  5. Correct history that was entered wrong. If a completed week was mistyped, its picks can be unlocked for editing — an entry shouldn't be eliminated over bad bookkeeping.
  6. Rehearse before the season. Test Mode works on a throwaway copy of the pool with synthetic results, so you can experience a full season of locks and eliminations without risking the real pool.

📣 Public-behavior presets

These presets model future opponent choices; they do not claim to know actual submitted picks. Every opponent is still restricted to their real unused teams from the pool grid. Within those choices, the model weights a team by its win probability raised to a preset power.

  • Chalk (win probability⁸) assumes entrants heavily converge on the safest available favorite. Use it when your group follows betting lines closely or tends to copy obvious picks. A winning lower-owned team can be valuable, but do not sacrifice too much win probability merely to be different.
  • Balanced (win probability³) assumes the field still prefers favorites but splits across several good options. This is a sensible default for a typical office pool with mixed experience; the gap between the safe and leverage recommendations should usually be modest.
  • Contrarian (win probability¹) assumes entrants spread out widely, including on riskier choices. Use it when people intentionally fade favorites or make casual picks. Differentiation is less scarce here, so the safest survival choice often deserves more weight.

If you have real current-week ownership, treat it as stronger evidence than a preset. This app does not import submitted future picks, so compare the projected ownership with how your specific pool actually behaves and re-run the calculation after changing the preset.

Calculations are manual because the season-path search and pool simulation can take a moment. Changes to the grid, blend slider, or public-behavior setting mark the advice stale; press Calculate strategy again to refresh it.

💹 Market (moneylines)

A moneyline is a pure "who wins" price. Decimal odds convert straight to probability: 1 ÷ 1.56 ≈ 64%. Books shade both sides ~4–5% as their commission (the vig), so we normalize them back to a fair 100% and average every book that posted a line. This is Vegas' collective opinion of win probability — including subtleties like NFL's key numbers of 3 and 7.

In ATS mode this is the engine: Cover % is built from the de-vigged moneyline, then compared against the posted spread to find which side beats the line.

📐 Model (spread + total)

Our independent estimate assumes final margins follow a bell curve centered on the average spread. Game randomness is ~13.5 points of standard deviation in an average scoring environment (44-point total) and grows with shootouts — so −7 in a 38-total game wins ~71% but only ~68% at a 56 total. Same spread, different risk.

In ATS mode the model can't call a cover on its own — it's derived from the line itself — so it only fills in when a game has no moneyline, and those games correctly read ~50% (no signal).

🎚️ The slider

Sets how much Market (sportsbook moneylines) vs Model (spread + total math) feeds the Win % column (default 50/50, saved in this browser). Games without posted moneylines always fall back to the model alone. When Market and Model disagree by a lot, the gap itself is worth a look before locking picks.

⚖️ Sportsbook weighting

When averaging lines across sportsbooks we don't treat every book equally. Regulated "sharp" books (DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM) get double weight — their lines move fast on sharp money. Offshore "soft" books (BetUS, MyBookie.ag, Bovada, LowVig, BetOnline) get half weight — they update slowly and carry wide vig with public bias. Any other book is neutral (×1). This keeps a stale soft line from dragging the consensus at equal weight to a fresh, sharp number.

🧮 The math behind the numbers

  1. Weighted consensus. Every bookmaker's spread and total are averaged into one number per game, using the quality weighting described above.
  2. De-vigged moneylines. Each book's decimal prices convert to implied probabilities (1 ÷ 1.91 ≈ 52%), but the two sides always sum to ~104% because of the vig. Dividing each by that sum strips the commission and returns fair probabilities; the favorite's share is then weighted-averaged across books. That fair number feeds everything below.
  3. Win probability. The favorite's margin is modeled as a bell curve: margin ~ Normal(spread, σ) with σ = 13.5 · √(total ÷ 44) points, so Win % = Φ(spread ÷ σ) — the area under that curve to the right of zero. Φ is the standard-normal CDF, computed with the error function.
  4. Blend & rank. Win % = W · model + (1 − W) · market, where W is your slider position. Games sort by Win % and take confidence points 16 → 1; ties fall back to straight-up odds.
  5. Implied margin. The moneyline probability is inverted back into points: μ = σ · Φ⁻¹(p). Φ⁻¹ is the inverse normal function (Acklam's approximation plus a Newton polish), and it answers: how many points would the market expect this favorite to win by?
  6. Cover probability & flip. Cover % = Φ((μ − spread) ÷ σ) for the favorite. Below 50% the moneyline says the spread gives away too many points, so the pick flips to the underdog (displayed as 1 − cover, with those rows reading (dog)). Games rank by Cover %.
